## What Is EMDR Therapy?

Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing, commonly known as
[EMDR](https://addictionandrecoverycenter.com/emdr-therapy/), is a structured
form of psychotherapy developed to help people process distressing memories
and reduce the emotional impact associated with traumatic experiences.

The therapy was developed by [psychologist Francine
Shapiro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francine_Shapiro) in the late 1980s. It
is now widely studied as a treatment for post-traumatic stress disorder and
other trauma-related difficulties.

Unlike some traditional forms of talk therapy, EMDR does not require
individuals to describe every detail of a traumatic experience repeatedly.
Instead, the therapist guides the person to briefly focus on selected aspects
of a distressing memory while simultaneously experiencing bilateral
stimulation.

Bilateral stimulation involves alternating stimulation between the left and
right sides of the body or sensory system. Common approaches include:

  * Following the therapist's hand movements with the eyes.
  * Listening to alternating sounds through headphones.
  * Feeling gentle alternating taps or vibrations.

The purpose is to support the processing of distressing memories so that they
may become less emotionally overwhelming.

EMDR is based partly on the Adaptive Information Processing (AIP) model. This
model proposes that some distressing experiences may remain inadequately
processed and continue to influence how a person responds to reminders of the
past.

The exact mechanisms through which EMDR produces its effects are still being
studied. However, research and clinical guidelines support its use as one of
the trauma-focused psychotherapies for PTSD.

## How Does EMDR Therapy Work?

After a traumatic experience, a person may continue to experience intense
emotional reactions when something reminds them of what happened.

For example, someone involved in a serious car accident may feel anxious when
hearing sudden braking sounds or approaching an intersection. Another person
who experienced abuse may become distressed by situations that remind them of
the original experience.

These reactions can occur even when the person understands intellectually that
the original danger is no longer present.

EMDR therapy aims to help individuals process the memories and associated
emotions that contribute to these reactions.

During a session, the therapist helps the individual identify a specific
memory, including the associated thoughts, emotions, and physical sensations.
The individual then focuses briefly on aspects of that memory while following
a guided pattern of bilateral stimulation.

The therapist periodically pauses to check what the person is experiencing and
guides the next steps according to the EMDR protocol.

As treatment progresses, the memory may become less vivid or emotionally
distressing. Individuals may also develop more balanced perspectives about
themselves and the experience.

For example, a person who previously believed, "I am not safe anywhere," may
gradually develop a more realistic understanding that the traumatic event
happened in the past and that they have ways to recognize and respond to
safety in the present.

The goal is not to erase the memory or pretend the event never happened. It is
to help the person remember the experience without experiencing the same level
of distress.

## The Eight Phases of EMDR Therapy

EMDR follows a structured eight-phase approach. Treatment is individualized,
and the time spent in each phase depends on the person's history, symptoms,
readiness, and clinical needs.

### Phase 1: History Taking and Treatment Planning

The therapist begins by learning about the individual's personal history,
current concerns, previous experiences, and treatment goals.

This includes identifying distressing memories, current situations that
trigger emotional reactions, and experiences that may be contributing to
present difficulties.

The therapist uses this information to develop an individualized treatment
plan and determine whether EMDR is appropriate.

### Phase 2: Preparation

Before processing traumatic memories, the therapist explains how EMDR works
and what the individual can expect during treatment.

An important part of this phase is helping the person develop emotional
regulation and coping strategies.

These may include:

  * Breathing and relaxation techniques.
  * Grounding exercises.
  * Visualization and calming imagery.
  * Techniques for managing distress between sessions.

Preparation helps establish a sense of safety and gives individuals tools to
manage difficult emotions that may arise during treatment.

### Phase 3: Assessment

During the assessment phase, the therapist and individual identify a specific
memory to work on.

They may explore:

  * The image or aspect of the experience that feels most distressing.
  * Negative beliefs associated with the memory.
  * Emotions connected to the experience.
  * Physical sensations that arise when remembering it.
  * A more helpful or positive belief the individual would like to develop.

The therapist may use standardized rating scales to assess the level of
distress and the strength of the desired positive belief.

### Phase 4: Desensitization

This phase involves processing the selected memory through bilateral
stimulation.

The individual briefly focuses on the distressing memory while following the
therapist's guided eye movements or another form of bilateral stimulation.

The therapist periodically pauses to allow the individual to report thoughts,
emotions, images, or physical sensations that arise.

The process continues according to the therapist's clinical judgment and the
individual's responses.

The aim is to reduce the emotional intensity associated with the targeted
memory.

### Phase 5: Installation

Once the distress associated with the memory has reduced sufficiently,
attention shifts toward strengthening a more adaptive or positive belief.

For example, someone who previously associated an accident with the belief "I
am powerless" may work toward a more balanced belief such as "I can take steps
to protect myself and move forward."

Bilateral stimulation may be used to support this phase.

### Phase 6: Body Scan

Traumatic memories can be associated with physical sensations such as tension,
tightness, or discomfort.

During the body scan, the therapist asks the individual to notice whether any
physical distress remains when thinking about the memory and the more positive
belief.

If discomfort remains, additional processing may be considered.

### Phase 7: Closure

At the end of a session, the therapist helps the individual return to a stable
emotional state.

If memory processing is incomplete, the therapist may use grounding or
relaxation techniques and discuss strategies for managing emotions between
sessions.

The individual may also be encouraged to notice any thoughts, dreams, or
reactions that arise before the next appointment.

### Phase 8: Reevaluation

At the beginning of subsequent sessions, the therapist reviews progress and
reassesses previously targeted memories.

They evaluate whether the distress has remained reduced, whether new concerns
have emerged, and whether additional treatment is needed.

This phase helps guide the next steps in the individual's treatment plan.

## How Can EMDR Therapy Help With Trauma Recovery?

Trauma recovery is not simply about forgetting a difficult experience. It
involves developing healthier ways to understand the past, respond to
reminders, regulate emotions, and participate in everyday life.

EMDR may support this process in several ways.

### 1\. Reducing the Emotional Intensity of Traumatic Memories

One of the primary goals of EMDR is to help reduce the emotional distress
associated with traumatic memories.

Someone who experienced a frightening accident, assault, or other distressing
event may continue to feel intense fear or anxiety when remembering it.

Through structured memory processing, EMDR may help the person remember the
experience without experiencing the same level of emotional overwhelm.

### 2\. Helping Manage PTSD Symptoms

Post-traumatic stress disorder can develop after exposure to a traumatic
event. Symptoms may include:

  * Intrusive memories or unwanted thoughts.
  * Nightmares or sleep disturbances.
  * Avoidance of people, places, or situations associated with the trauma.
  * Feeling constantly alert or on guard.
  * Strong emotional reactions to reminders.
  * Negative beliefs about oneself or the world.

EMDR is one of the trauma-focused psychotherapies recommended in major
clinical practice guidelines for PTSD.

Treatment may help reduce the distress associated with traumatic memories and
improve a person's ability to manage reminders of the event.

### 3\. Addressing Negative Beliefs About Yourself

Traumatic experiences can sometimes contribute to persistent negative beliefs.

A person may begin to think:

  * "It was my fault."
  * "I am not safe."
  * "I cannot trust anyone."
  * "I am powerless."
  * "Something is wrong with me."

These beliefs can affect self-esteem, relationships, and everyday decision-
making.

EMDR includes a phase that focuses on developing and strengthening more
adaptive beliefs. The aim is to help individuals develop a healthier
perspective about themselves and what happened.

### 4\. Improving Emotional Regulation

Some people experience intense emotional reactions that feel difficult to
control after trauma.

They may become overwhelmed by anxiety, fear, anger, sadness, or reminders of
the past.

By helping individuals process distressing memories and develop coping
resources, EMDR may support improved emotional regulation.

The goal is not to eliminate normal emotions but to help individuals respond
to difficult situations with greater stability and flexibility.

### 5\. Reducing Avoidance and Trigger Responses

Avoidance is a common response to trauma.

An individual may avoid driving, certain locations, specific conversations, or
situations that remind them of a distressing event.

Although avoidance may provide temporary relief, it can also interfere with
everyday activities and relationships.

EMDR may help reduce the distress associated with certain
[trauma](https://addictionandrecoverycenter.com/trauma-therapy/) reminders,
making it easier for individuals to approach situations that previously
triggered strong reactions.

### 6\. Supporting Better Sleep and Daily Functioning

Trauma-related symptoms can interfere with sleep, concentration, work, and
relationships.

Nightmares, intrusive thoughts, and heightened alertness may make it difficult
to relax or feel comfortable in everyday environments.

When EMDR helps reduce trauma-related distress, some individuals may also
experience improvements in sleep and daily functioning.

However, results vary, and additional treatment may be necessary when sleep
problems or other symptoms have separate contributing causes.

## What Types of Trauma Can EMDR Address?

EMDR was initially developed for PTSD and is particularly associated with
processing traumatic memories.

Depending on the individual's circumstances and the therapist's assessment,
EMDR may be considered for distress associated with different experiences.

Examples include:

**Motor Vehicle Accidents:** A serious accident may leave someone with
persistent fear of driving, intrusive memories, or anxiety when traveling.

**Physical or Emotional Abuse:** Experiences involving abuse may contribute to
long-term emotional distress, difficulty trusting others, and negative beliefs
about oneself.

**Sexual Assault:** EMDR may be used as part of trauma-focused treatment for
individuals experiencing distress related to sexual violence.

**Childhood Trauma:** Neglect, abuse, or other adverse childhood experiences
may continue to influence emotional well-being and relationships in adulthood.

**Violence or Threatening Experiences:** Witnessing or experiencing violence
may contribute to persistent fear, hypervigilance, or trauma-related symptoms.

**Sudden Loss or Distressing Life Events:** Some individuals experience
significant distress following unexpected or overwhelming events. A therapist
can assess whether EMDR is appropriate for the concerns involved.

Not every difficult or upsetting experience leads to PTSD, and not every
person who experiences trauma needs EMDR. Treatment should be based on an
appropriate clinical assessment.

## Is EMDR Therapy Effective for PTSD?

EMDR is supported by a substantial body of research and is recognized in
several major clinical practice guidelines as an effective trauma-focused
psychotherapy for PTSD.

The U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense include EMDR
among their recommended individual trauma-focused psychotherapies for PTSD,
alongside Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT) and Prolonged Exposure (PE).

The American Psychological Association also discusses EMDR as a treatment
option for PTSD.

Research has found that EMDR can reduce PTSD symptoms for many individuals.
However, outcomes vary, and no treatment works equally well for everyone.

It is also important to understand that EMDR is not the only evidence-based
approach to trauma recovery. Other treatments, including CPT and Prolonged
Exposure, may also be appropriate.

A qualified mental health professional can help determine which approach best
fits an individual's symptoms, preferences, history, and treatment goals.

## EMDR Therapy vs. Traditional Talk Therapy

Both EMDR and traditional talk therapy can play important roles in mental
health treatment, but their approaches are different.

Traditional talk therapy may involve discussing experiences, identifying
thought patterns, exploring emotions, and developing coping strategies.

EMDR focuses more directly on processing selected distressing memories through
a structured protocol that includes bilateral stimulation.

Here are some general differences:

Feature| EMDR Therapy| Traditional Talk Therapy  
---|---|---  
Primary focus| Processing distressing memories| Exploring thoughts, emotions,
behaviors, and experiences  
Main approach| Structured memory processing with bilateral stimulation|
Conversation and therapeutic techniques  
Trauma discussion| Does not generally require lengthy verbal descriptions of
every traumatic detail| May involve discussing experiences in greater detail,
depending on the approach  
Treatment structure| Eight phases| Varies by therapy type  
Treatment goals| Reduce distress associated with traumatic memories and
develop adaptive beliefs| Improve emotional well-being, coping, behavior, and
understanding  
  
Neither approach is automatically better for every person. The most
appropriate treatment depends on the individual's needs and the therapist's
clinical recommendations.

## Can EMDR Help With Anxiety and Depression?

Some individuals experience anxiety and depressive symptoms alongside trauma-
related difficulties.

For example, unresolved trauma may be associated with persistent worry,
feelings of helplessness, low self-worth, emotional distress, or difficulty
enjoying everyday activities.

EMDR may be considered when these concerns are connected to distressing
memories or trauma-related symptoms.

However, EMDR is not a universal treatment for every anxiety or depressive
disorder. People experiencing these conditions may benefit from other
evidence-based therapies, medication, or a combination of approaches.

A comprehensive mental health assessment can help identify the underlying
concerns and guide treatment planning.

## Can EMDR Help People With Addiction and Substance Use Disorders?

Trauma and substance use can sometimes occur together.

Some individuals use alcohol or drugs as a way to cope with difficult
emotions, intrusive memories, anxiety, or other psychological distress. While
substances may temporarily change how a person feels, they can also contribute
to additional emotional, physical, and social difficulties.

When trauma and substance use occur together, addressing both concerns may be
important for long-term recovery.

EMDR may be considered as part of an individualized treatment plan when a
qualified clinician determines that trauma-related symptoms are contributing
to a person's difficulties.

However, EMDR is not a standalone treatment for substance use disorders and
should not replace appropriate addiction treatment.

Depending on the person's needs, a comprehensive treatment plan may include:

  * Substance-use assessment and treatment planning.
  * Individual or group counseling.
  * Evidence-based addiction treatment.
  * Relapse-prevention strategies.
  * Treatment for co-occurring mental health conditions.
  * Trauma-focused therapy when clinically appropriate.
  * Medication-based treatment when indicated.

Treatment should address both substance use and relevant mental health
concerns rather than assuming that treating one automatically resolves the
other.

## What Should You Expect During an EMDR Therapy Session?

Your first EMDR appointment may involve more discussion and assessment than
actual memory processing.

The therapist will generally want to understand your history, current
symptoms, treatment goals, and concerns before deciding whether EMDR is
appropriate.

During later sessions, the therapist may guide you through the processing of
selected memories using eye movements, alternating sounds, or tapping.

You remain awake and aware throughout the process. Your therapist will
periodically check how you are feeling and whether you are ready to continue.

People can have different experiences during EMDR. Some may notice changes in
emotions, thoughts, memories, or physical sensations. Others may experience
discomfort or temporary emotional activation while processing difficult
material.

A trained therapist should monitor your responses, adjust the process when
necessary, and help you maintain emotional stability.

You should feel comfortable discussing concerns, asking questions, and
communicating if you need a pause.

## How Long Does EMDR Therapy Take?

There is no single treatment timeline that applies to everyone.

Some individuals may experience meaningful improvement after a relatively
short course of treatment, while others may need more sessions because of
complex trauma, multiple distressing experiences, or additional mental health
concerns.

Factors that can influence treatment duration include:

  * The number and nature of traumatic experiences.
  * The severity of current symptoms.
  * Whether trauma occurred repeatedly over an extended period.
  * The presence of other mental health conditions.
  * The individual's readiness and ability to manage distress.
  * Progress during treatment.

The American Psychological Association describes EMDR as commonly delivered
one to two times per week over approximately 6 to 12 sessions, although actual
treatment duration varies.

Your therapist should discuss a realistic treatment plan based on your
individual circumstances rather than promise a specific recovery timeline.

## Are There Side Effects or Risks of EMDR Therapy?

EMDR is generally considered an appropriate treatment option for many people
with PTSD when administered by a properly trained mental health professional.

However, processing distressing memories can temporarily bring up difficult
emotions or physical sensations.

Some individuals may experience:

  * Emotional discomfort during or after a session.
  * Temporary increases in distress.
  * Fatigue.
  * Vivid dreams.
  * Memories or thoughts that continue between appointments.

These experiences should be discussed with the treating therapist.

EMDR may need to be adapted, delayed, or incorporated into a broader treatment
plan depending on the individual's mental health, stability, and ability to
manage emotional distress.

People experiencing severe dissociation, active safety concerns, significant
instability, or other complex symptoms may need additional assessment and
preparation before memory processing begins.

Do not attempt to perform trauma processing on yourself using online eye-
movement videos or unsupervised techniques. EMDR is a structured psychotherapy
that should be delivered by a properly trained clinician.

## How to Find a Qualified EMDR Therapist

Choosing an appropriately trained therapist is an important part of the
treatment process.

EMDR involves more than simply following eye movements. It requires clinical
assessment, treatment planning, preparation, memory processing, and ongoing
evaluation.

When considering EMDR, ask potential providers:

  1. Have you completed formal EMDR training?
  2. What experience do you have treating trauma and PTSD?
  3. Do you work with individuals who have experienced complex or repeated trauma?
  4. How do you determine whether EMDR is appropriate for a client?
  5. What happens if I become overwhelmed during a session?
  6. How do you evaluate treatment progress?
  7. Do you coordinate care when someone has co-occurring mental health or substance-use concerns?

You can also use the EMDR International Association's therapist directory to
investigate qualified EMDR providers.

The right therapist should explain the treatment process clearly, respect your
boundaries, and develop a plan that reflects your needs.

## Frequently Asked Questions About EMDR Therapy

### What does EMDR stand for?

EMDR stands for Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing. It is a
structured psychotherapy that uses bilateral stimulation while an individual
focuses on selected distressing memories.

### What is the main purpose of EMDR therapy?

The primary purpose of EMDR is to help individuals process distressing
memories and reduce the emotional intensity associated with them. It is
commonly used in the treatment of PTSD and other trauma-related difficulties.

### Does EMDR make you relive trauma?

EMDR involves briefly focusing on aspects of a distressing memory, but it does
not generally require individuals to describe every detail repeatedly. A
trained therapist guides the process and helps manage emotional distress.

### Does EMDR work for PTSD?

Research supports EMDR as an effective trauma-focused psychotherapy for many
individuals with PTSD. It is recommended in major clinical guidelines,
including those from the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs and Department of
Defense.

However, treatment outcomes vary, and a qualified professional should
determine whether EMDR is appropriate.

### How many EMDR sessions are usually needed?

Some individuals may benefit from a relatively short course of treatment,
while others require more sessions. The number depends on the nature of the
trauma, symptom severity, treatment goals, and individual progress.

### Can EMDR help with childhood trauma?

EMDR may be used to address distress associated with childhood abuse, neglect,
and other traumatic experiences. Treatment should be individualized,
particularly when trauma was repeated or occurred over an extended period.

### Is EMDR better than talk therapy?

Neither treatment is universally better. EMDR and other forms of psychotherapy
use different approaches. The appropriate option depends on the individual's
symptoms, preferences, clinical history, and treatment goals.

### Can EMDR help with anxiety?

EMDR may help when anxiety is connected to traumatic memories or distressing
experiences. However, it is not necessarily the most appropriate treatment for
every anxiety disorder.

### Can EMDR help with addiction?

EMDR may be considered when trauma-related symptoms and substance use occur
together. It can form part of a broader treatment plan but should not replace
evidence-based addiction treatment.

### Is EMDR therapy safe?

EMDR is used by trained mental health professionals as a trauma-focused
treatment. Some individuals experience temporary emotional discomfort or
fatigue during the process. A qualified therapist should assess readiness and
monitor the person's response.

### Can EMDR be done online?

EMDR may be delivered through telehealth by appropriately trained clinicians
when clinically suitable. The therapist should determine whether remote
treatment is appropriate and ensure that the necessary support and procedures
are in place.
